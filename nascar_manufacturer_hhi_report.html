Method

The Index, in plain terms

The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index is the standard concentration measure in antitrust economics, and it maps onto sports parity questions almost without modification. For each decade, take every manufacturer's share of championships, square each share, and sum:

HHI formula

HHI = Σ (market sharei × 100)²

Scale runs 0–10,000. A decade split evenly across five manufacturers scores 2,000; a decade owned by one manufacturer scores 10,000. I'm using the convention regulators use for merger review: below 1,500 is unconcentrated, 1,500–2,500 is moderately concentrated, above 2,500 is highly concentrated. I also report the "effective number of manufacturers" (10,000 / HHI) — a more intuitive read on how many automakers a decade felt like it had, competitively.

Data quirk — how I handled it

18 of the 68 championship seasons have more than one row: the champion drove different manufacturers' cars within the same season (car-number and manufacturer swaps were common before teams settled into exclusive factory deals). None of these seasons had a tie for most wins, so I attributed each title to whichever manufacturer that champion won the most races with that year — the car they actually won in, not just started the season in. Notably, every multi-manufacturer season falls at or before 1983; after that, championship-winning drivers stuck with one manufacturer for the full year, which is itself a small signal of the sport professionalizing and factory deals solidifying.

The Index over time

Wide open in the '60s, locked up in the '90s

Concentration was never actually low by regulatory standards — NASCAR's championship has always leaned on a small handful of manufacturers — but there's a clear arc: a genuinely competitive mid-century trough, a steady climb through the '70s and '80s, a sharp peak in the 1990s, and a partial, incomplete recovery after that.

Composition

What the concentration is made of

The HHI number hides who is winning. Laid out by manufacturer, three separate stories are visible: the mid-century era genuinely split between five brands (several now defunct), a long Chevrolet ascent, and a shrinking field of entrants as manufacturers consolidated or exited altogether.

The story

Does parity track anything real?

01

The '60s were the genuinely competitive era

The lowest HHI in the dataset (2,200) falls in the 1960s, when five manufacturers — Ford, Chevrolet, Plymouth, Pontiac, and Dodge — split titles with no single brand taking more than 30%. This is also the era right after manufacturers began providing serious factory support (mid-1960s), so more automakers were funding competitive programs simultaneously than at almost any other point in the sport's history. Wide-open competition and broad manufacturer investment arrived together.

02

Every manufacturer exit shows up as a concentration spike

Concentration climbs almost in lockstep with the shrinking manufacturer field. Plymouth pulled factory support after 1977; Mercury and the first-generation Dodge program left in the early '80s; by 1991 GM alone was still fielding four brands (Chevrolet, Pontiac, Oldsmobile, Buick), but Oldsmobile and Buick were gone from the grid within three years. That consolidation lands squarely inside the 1990s — the decade where HHI peaks at 6,800 and the "effective number of manufacturers" collapses to 1.47, i.e. a near-duopoly between Chevrolet (80% of titles) and Ford. Fewer manufacturers funding programs isn't just correlated with higher concentration here — it's mechanically most of the explanation.

03

New entrants only partly undid it

Pontiac's championship contention in the 2000s and Toyota's arrival in 2007 (the first new manufacturer since 1971) both nudge HHI back down — the 2000s reading (4,400) is meaningfully lower than the 1990s peak. But the recovery is partial, not a return to '60s-style parity: Chevrolet has taken at least 50% of titles in every decade since the 1980s. Adding a competitor lowers concentration; it doesn't reset it.

04

The trend through the end of this dataset: not toward parity

Within the window this data covers, competitive balance never returns to '60s levels. The 2010s (2010–2016 only) sit at 5,510 — higher than the 2000s, driven by Chevrolet winning 5 of 7 titles and only Dodge and Toyota (one apiece) breaking through. That's a partial decade, so it's a soft read, but the direction is toward re-concentration, not away from it, right up to where the data stops.

Beyond the dataset

This dataset ends in 2016, so the "is it trending toward more balance recently" question can only be answered up to that point from the numbers above. For context on what came after: Dodge had already exited after 2012 (still visible in this data), and Chevrolet went on to win five consecutive Cup titles from 2021 through 2025, per NASCAR manufacturer-standings records[1] — which would push a fully updated 2020s HHI toward the highest concentration in series history, extending Finding 4 rather than reversing it. I haven't built that into the index above since I don't have full manufacturer win data for 2017–2025 in this dataset, but it's worth flagging for anyone extending this analysis.
